1The idea in one page

Sensors are strapped to a worker. They record how the body is positioned, many times a second, for the whole recording. The phone shows live feedback. Afterwards the data is uploaded, the backend works out how much time was spent in each posture, and a report gives a green, yellow or red verdict.

An angle is never dangerous by itself. It becomes dangerous when it is held for too much of the working day.

Raising your arm to 70° is fine. Raising it to 70° for three hours is not. Every risk colour in the product is really answering one question: what share of the recording was spent in the bad position?

That single idea explains almost everything else. The angles decide which bad position. The percentages decide how bad. The assessments (RAMP, MEC, RULA, REBA) then wrap extra judgement around it — weights being lifted, how often a motion repeats, what the worker says hurts.

The chain, start to finish

1. SensorsMeasure orientation many times a second
2. CalibrationEstablishes what “neutral” means for this worker
3. AnglesEach reading becomes an angle for each body part
4. RangesEach angle falls into a range, e.g. 30–60°
5. Time shareHow much of the recording was spent in each range
6. ColourThe time share is compared against a limit → green, yellow or red

How an angle is worked out

For the arm, gravity is enough. The sensor always knows which way is down. Calibration recorded which way down pointed when the arm hung by the side. During work, the sensor compares where down is now with where it was then — and the difference between those two directions is the elevation angle.

For the trunk and head, gravity is not enough. Here we need two separate numbers: forward bending and side bending. Gravity alone cannot tell them apart — tilting 20° forward and tilting 20° sideways shift gravity by the same amount. To know which, you need to know which way the body is facing. So for these we track full three-dimensional orientation, combining the gravity sensor with a rotation sensor.

Arms need one number, so gravity is enough. Trunk and head need two, so they need orientation.

Speed is measured in degrees per second, not metres per second

Because we measure rotation, not travel. The sensor does not know where the arm is in the room, only which way it is pointing. As a rule of thumb, 30 °/s is about the speed of raising your arm from your side to horizontal in three seconds.

Worth knowing

Arm speed is worked out two different ways in the product. The reports use the rate at which the arm rises and falls, so purely sideways movement counts as zero. The phone uses total rotation in every direction. The two will never produce the same number for the same session.

3Calibration — why 0° means anything

A sensor on its own only knows which way gravity pulls. It has no idea where the body is. Calibration is what connects the two.

The worker stands still in a neutral pose for six seconds. The app records the sensor’s orientation and stores it as “zero”. Every angle afterwards is measured relative to that moment.

If the worker moves too much during those six seconds the calibration is rejected and they are asked to redo it. The app watches how much the readings wobble and refuses anything above a set amount.

Without calibration, every angle in the recording is meaningless.

This is the single biggest thing a user can get wrong. A sloppy calibration does not produce an error later — it produces a report full of confident, wrong numbers.

4How we decide it’s dangerous

Always two steps. This is the core of the whole product.

Step 1 — sort every moment into a range

Body partRanges

Trunk forwardExtension (past −5°)  ·  −5 to 10°  ·  10–20°  ·  20–45°  ·  above 45°
Head forwardExtension (past −5°)  ·  −5 to 10°  ·  10–20°  ·  20–30°  ·  above 30°
Side bending (trunk & head)0–10°  ·  10–30°  ·  above 30°
Arm speed0–15  ·  15–30  ·  above 30 °/s
Wrist speed0–10  ·  10–20  ·  above 20 °/s
How to read a range

30° – 60° means more than 30, up to and including 60. The lower edge is excluded, the upper edge is included. So a reading of exactly 30.0° falls in the range below, and exactly 60.0° falls in this one. If a value sits precisely on a boundary, it goes up into the higher range.

Deliberate, not a mistake

The range shown as “0° – 10°” for the head and trunk actually covers −5° to 10°. The −5° edge absorbs sensor and calibration noise, so a worker standing upright is not wrongly recorded as leaning backwards. This is intentional.

Red on “Arm above 60°” does not mean the arm went above 60°.

It means the arm was above 60° for more than 10% of the recording. This is the single most common misreading of our reports.

A limitation to be aware of

These are percentages of the recording, with no adjustment for how long the recording was. The published research behind these numbers gives limits for an eight-hour workday, and the method assumes a recording is a representative sample of the shift. If it is not — a very short recording, or one taken during an unusual task — the percentages will still be calculated but the verdict will not mean what it appears to mean.

5Percentiles, medians and averages

Reports are full of “P50” and “P90”. They are simpler than they look.

A percentile is: sort every reading from smallest to largest, then point at a position in that line.

The sensors take roughly thirteen readings a second, so a ten-minute recording produces about eight thousand angle values. Sort all eight thousand from smallest to largest, and:

P10One tenth of the way along. The relaxed end.
P50The middle. Half the readings are below it. “P50” and “median” are two names for the same number.
P90Nine tenths of the way along. One reading in ten is higher than this.
P99Almost the very end. Near the worst moment of the recording.

Why not just use the average?

Ten readings of arm angle

5, 8, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 25, 60, 80

Average = 25.3° — dragged upward by the two big values. Misleading: the arm was below 25° for eight of the ten readings.

Median (P50) = 16.5° — “normally the arm sits around 16°.”

P90 ≈ 60° — “one tenth of the time the arm was at 60° or above.”

Injury comes from sustained high exposure, and the average hides it. Two workers can have an identical average — one holding a steady 20° all day, another swinging between 0° and 40° — while their shoulders are under completely different loads. Percentiles separate them.

P50 is the posture they work in normally. P90 is the posture they reach when it gets bad.

If P50 looks fine but P90 is high, the job has bad moments inside an otherwise acceptable task. If P50 is already high, the whole task is wrong.

An important thing percentiles do not do

If the entire recording was spent in safe angles, then even P99 will be a safe angle — and that is the correct answer. It tells you the worker never went extreme, not even in their worst 1%.

Percentiles answer “how high did it get?”. The risk colours answer “how long was it too high?”. Two different questions, and the product uses both.

Three workers
WorkerP50P90P99Time above 60°Verdict
Arm at 20° all shift20°22°25°0%Safe

6What the worker sees live

During recording, each body part has a tile that changes colour. There are two feedback modes.

Instant — reacts to the current moment

Body partGreyOrangeRed
Arm elevationup to 30°30–60°above 60°
Arm speed0–30 °/s30–60 °/sabove 60 °/s
Trunk / head bendingup to 20°20–45°above 45°
Heart rateAlways grey — never coloured during recording

Accumulated — reacts to how the session is going so far

A second mode that builds up over the recording. For arms it turns orange once more than 25% of the time has been spent above 30° (or more than 5% above 60°), and red at more than 50% and 10%. Those are the same shapes as the risk model in section 4.

Live feedback and the report do not always agree

The phone holds its own copies of these thresholds, and several have drifted from the ones the reports use. A worker can see green all day for a posture the report later flags. This is documented in the companion issues document — treat live feedback as guidance, and the report as the verdict.

RAMP

Risk Assessment and Management tool for manual handling, Proactively. Developed at KTH in Sweden. Seven sections: postures, movements and repetition, lifting, pushing and pulling, influencing factors, strenuous work, and the worker’s perceived discomfort.

Postures 1 to 6 are filled in automatically from the sensors. Everything else — weights, forces, environment, what the worker says hurts — is entered by a human assessor.

MEC

Fully sensor-derived — no human input at all. It asks how often, and for how long, the worker sits in a demanding position. Everything is expressed per hour, so recordings of different lengths can be compared.

Two numbers per question:

  • Movements per hour — how often the motion repeats
  • Static minutes per hour — how long the position is held still
ArmsGreenYellowRed
Movements per hourup to 1515–60over 60
Static minutes per hourup to 44–8over 8

MEC also works out where the hand is in space without a sensor on the hand. It treats the arm as two rigid sticks — upper arm and forearm — and uses the two measured angles plus the limb lengths to calculate how far the hand is from the body. That is how it can report “more than 30 cm” or “more than 50 cm”.

RULA and REBA

Entirely manual. No sensor input. An assessor scores each body part by eye, adds points for muscle use and load, then works through three lookup tables to reach a final score.

RULA scoreMeaning
1–2Acceptable

5–6Investigate and change soon
7Investigate and change immediately

8TNO — holding versus moving

Named after the Dutch research institute. It answers a question the other measurements do not: was the worker holding a position, or moving through it? Holding still and moving repeatedly cause different kinds of strain.

How it works:

  1. Find the peaks and troughs in the angle signal over time
  2. Group them into periods — a stretch where the angle stays within about 5°
  3. A period lasting 6 seconds or more is counted as static (holding); anything shorter is dynamic (moving)
  4. Report static as a share of time, and dynamic as movements per minute — each broken down by the angle range it happened in
Read the companion issues document before using TNO figures

A defect in the range-sorting means some angle ranges are never counted. This is the highest-priority item on the issues list and it affects every TNO figure the product has produced.

A trap in the line chart

The line chart shows one point per minute, not per reading. A three-second reach overhead almost disappears into that minute’s average. Use the bar chart or the percentage tables to find peaks — the line chart shows trends, not spikes.

Multi-session comparison places sessions side by side as columns. It does not merge them into one combined figure.

10Heart rate

The heart rate block is the most jargon-heavy part of any report. All of it comes from a standard, well-established analysis library rather than our own maths.

A healthy heart does not beat like a metronome.

The tiny variations between beats show the nervous system adapting. More variation means more recovery capacity. Less variation means more stress or fatigue. Almost every number below is a different way of measuring that variation.

TermWhat it meansDirection
Mean RRAverage milliseconds between two heartbeatsLower = working harder

RMSSDBeat-to-beat variation, short termHigher is better — recovery

If you only remember two: RMSSD for recovery, CSI for stress.

The product also calculates how long the worker was above a third of their heart-rate capacity (capacity being 220 minus their age, minus their resting rate) and how much genuine recovery time they got — stretches of five minutes or more back near resting.

Worth knowing

None of the heart-rate numbers have risk thresholds. They are shown as raw values with no colour, so a customer has no way to know whether their figure is good or bad from the report alone.

11Where our numbers come from

The thresholds are not invented. The product cites:

SourceWhat it gives us
Arvidsson et al. (2017), AMM Syd, SwedenArm limits and the arm velocity threshold
Hansson et al. (2016)The eight-hour recommended limits shown as reference values
Lind et al. (2020)Trunk limits
Rose, Lind & Forsman (2024), KTHThe RAMP II method itself
Forsman et al. (2022)Converting IMU sensor readings into comparable posture measurements

Two deliberate deviations, both documented in the report

  1. Trunk backward bending. The original RAMP method uses 0°. We use −10°, because of sensor sensitivity and our calibration method, to avoid overstating backward-bending risk.
  2. Arm velocity. The original AMM Syd method uses 60 °/s. We use 30 °/s, because IMU sensors estimate speed more accurately than the accelerometer-only sensors the original research used.

Both are printed in the Word report where a customer can read them. That is good practice and worth preserving.

Not traceable

The heart-rate thresholds, the recovery percentages and the TNO cut-offs have no published source cited anywhere in the product. They may well be sound, but we currently cannot show where they came from if asked.

12Things worth knowing

Short answers to questions that come up repeatedly.

QuestionAnswer
Do we measure head side bending?Yes, in the same ranges as the trunk. But it has no risk band of its own — it only feeds a RAMP score
Do we measure speed for every body part?Arms and wrist have risk bands. Trunk and head speed is recorded and shown, but no threshold is applied to it
Is the risk model configurable per customer?No. The posture thresholds are fixed in code. MEC alone lets the assessor set a few sensitivity parameters
Does the risk depend on the worker’s age or weight?Only for heart rate, where capacity is calculated from age. Posture thresholds are the same for everyone
Which assessments use the sensors?MEC entirely; RAMP for postures 1 to 6 only; RULA and REBA not at all
What happens to negative arm angles?They are removed before the distribution and risk calculations, but not before the percentiles
Are left and right side bending separated?No. The size of the lean is used and the direction is discarded
Why does the phone sometimes disagree with the report?The phone keeps its own copies of the thresholds, and several have drifted. See the companion issues document
